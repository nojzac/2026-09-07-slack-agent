SLACK AGENTS · CHAPTER "USING YOUR AGENT" · LESSON 19

On-call agents: let a channel trigger the bot

Anthropic put Claude on the front of their CI/CD alerts: an alert lands in a Slack channel, Claude investigates it before a human reads it, and only pages a person when it decides a person is needed. Ray walks through their write-up and then asks his own agent what abilities it would need to do the same. Nothing is built here — because for this bot the pattern is not an addition, it is the deliberate removal of two loop guards.

Ray's video: 6 min 28 s This page: 25 min At the end: a decision on whether Joestar reacts to an alert channel, and the exact guard changes it would take

What you'll have at the end

The pattern in one picture. The red band is the part Ray's video does not have to think about and this bot does.

  • The pattern named exactly: the trigger moves from a human mention to a message arriving.
  • The two guard exceptions written out, so if you build it you cut a narrow hole rather than a wide one.
  • A written answer on the Sentry-style MCP question, which this project's rules settle before it is asked.
  • A decision recorded in RESUME.md. For this workshop, that decision is "understood, deferred" — there is no alert stream yet.

Who this is for, and what it assumes

You have a bot that already hears every message in the channels it is in and deliberately ignores almost all of them — that behaviour arrived in lesson 07. You know how a GitHub token is minted per channel from the channel topic (lesson 08) and how an MCP server was assessed before being added (lesson 10). You do not need an alert stream to read this page; you need one to build it.

Why this lesson exists

Lesson 19 changes what starts a run. Lesson 20 changes what a run is for. They are usually built together.

The gap this closes. Ray shows himself typing "Percy, investigate this" under an alert and getting a good answer — and then says the quiet part: "the problem here is I have to manually tag my agent". A first responder that needs to be summoned is not a first responder.

Before you begin

An unprompted reply loop is the one failure this bot has an emergency procedure for. RESUME.md: a second unprompted reply means turning off the message.channels subscription in Slack immediately, then fixing the guard. Everything in this lesson pushes in that direction, so nothing here ships without a test that proves the bot ignores its own posts in the allowlisted channel.
  • Ray's two sources: Anthropic's post at claude.com/blog/ai-ci-cd-on-call and the starter kit at github.com/anthropics/oncall-kit.
  • An honest answer to "what pages me today?" — if the answer is nothing, this lesson ends at a written deferral.
PART 1

Understand what you're building

Three things: the pattern itself, the parts of it that are about detection rather than response, and what it would cost this particular bot to adopt.

The pattern: the message is the triggerWhat Anthropic run internally, as Ray summarises it.

UNDERSTAND

Alerts already land in Slack — every hosting, error-tracking and CI service offers it, and Ray demonstrates the setup on Trigger.dev in about thirty seconds. The pattern adds one thing: the agent watches a nominated channel and runs its triage prompt on every message that arrives, instead of waiting to be tagged.

A duty nurse, not a doctor you phone. Nobody decides the patient is worth a doctor's time; the patient walking in is the decision. Triage is the job, and escalation is one of its outcomes rather than its precondition.

The three parts worth stealing

PartWhat it means
A written escalation ruleAn on-call.md the agent reads: when to page a human now, when to investigate and leave a summary for the morning. The judgement is made once, in advance, not per incident
Parallel investigationFan out subagents across logs, metrics and recent changes at once rather than in sequence, because the clock is running
Provisioning a spaceAnthropic's agent creates a channel for an incident, posts its report there, and invites the people whose change is implicated
Ray's second, less obvious point: this changes detection, not only response. "Claude doesn't just transform how you respond to incidents, it transforms how you detect them." Thresholds nobody could set correctly — is a 1% error rate bad? — can be derived from your own history and re-tuned as it moves. That is an ownership job, which is lesson 20.
Ray's method for the whole chapter: paste the article or the video transcript to your agent and ask it what abilities it would need. His came back asking to create channels, to reply without a mention, for a webhook on channel messages, for read access to several services, and to write itself a triage skill. That list is a good starting point and a terrible finishing point — every item on it is a capability granted to everyone who can message the bot.

What this would cost Joestar, line by lineFour concrete obstacles, three of them specific to how this bot was built.

UNDERSTAND

1 · Alerts trip both loop guards

A monitoring alert is posted by an app, in a channel, addressed to nobody. classifyEvent() skips it twice over: "posted by a bot — never answer ourselves" and "channel chatter, not addressed to us". An on-call reflex needs both relaxed, and the narrow version is an allowlist: a named set of channels in which channel chatter runs, plus a named set of app ids whose posts are allowed to trigger — never a blanket "answer bots", which is how a bot ends up in conversation with itself.

The self-reply check must survive whatever you change. Joestar posts its own findings into the same channel. If the exception is written as "allow bot messages in #alerts", the bot's own message qualifies. The check on its own user id stays unconditional; only other, named apps get through.

2 · The sandbox is destroyed, so nothing accumulates

Every run gets a fresh E2B machine and there is no session to resume — context comes from replaying the Slack thread as untrusted data. "The same error showed up three times today" is therefore not something the bot knows; it is something it must be able to look up, in the thread, in the memory repo from lesson 15, or in the alerting service itself.

3 · An alerts channel has no repo in its topic, so there is no token

This bot mints a GitHub token per channel, scoped to the repository named in the channel topic. An #alerts channel with no topic gets no token — the investigation can read Slack and think, but cannot read the code or open the fix PR. Setting the topic is the fix, and it is also the moment to notice you have just granted write access to that repo to everyone who can post in the alerts channel.

4 · Every message becomes a run, and runs cost

The acknowledgement path already makes up to four Slack calls before returning 200, inside Slack's ~3-second window, and each run boots a sandbox and a model. Turning a busy alerts channel into a trigger multiplies that by the alert volume. A filter — severity, or first occurrence only — belongs in the guard, not in the prompt, because a prompt-level filter has already paid for the run.

Two abilities on Ray's list this project's rules already refuseDecided in advance, so they are not re-argued at the moment they seem convenient.

UNDERSTAND
A production monitoring MCP server — Sentry, Datadog, the hosting dashboard. CLAUDE.md: an MCP server is added only if handing its full capability to any workspace member would be acceptable. Production error data is not read-only public data, and being read-only does not rescue it: every member who can message the bot would get the same view. Until per-user scoping exists, the investigation reads what arrives in the alert message, or it happens by hand.
Creating channels and inviting people. Anthropic's version provisions a channel per incident. That needs conversations:write and the ability to invite members — which means anyone who can message the bot can have channels created and people pulled into them. Not forbidden by any existing rule, but it is a real grant, and it earns its own decision rather than riding along in an "on-call abilities" pull request.
What survives the rules comfortably. Reading the alert text, reasoning about it, reading the repo named in the channel topic, opening a pull request with a proposed fix, and posting a summary that tags a human. That is most of the value, and it is all inside what Joestar can already do.
WHAT PART 1 SETTLEDOn-call is a guard change plus an escalation rule, not a new feature. Two of the abilities the agent would ask for are refused here before it asks.
PART 2

Decide

No code was written for this lesson in this workshop. These are the steps you run when there is an alert stream worth reacting to.

Work out whether you have an alert stream, then scope the hole in the guardFour steps, in order. Step 1 ends the lesson for most people, honestly.

DO NOW

  1. List what actually alerts you today. Hosting failures, CI runs, error tracking, inbound leads, support mail. If the list is empty — as it is for this workshop, where nothing is in production — write "understood, deferred, revisit when anything is live" in RESUME.md and stop. That is a finished lesson.

    Where this departs from Ray. Ray has Sentry and Trigger.dev posting continuously, so he can skip this step. Building the reflex first and hoping for traffic means testing it with fake alerts, which tests the plumbing and none of the judgement.

  2. Pick one channel and one app, not "bots". The design is an explicit allowlist of channel ids, and an explicit allowlist of the app ids whose messages may trigger a run. Write both lists down before prompting; they are the whole security story of this feature.

  3. Write the escalation rule before the ability. An on-call.md, or a skill in the toolkit from lesson 14: what counts as page-a-human-now, what counts as investigate-and-report, and what counts as ignore. A bot given a trigger and no escalation rule pages you for everything, which is the situation you were trying to leave.

  4. Ask for it as one narrow pull request, and review it against the checklist. Does the bot still refuse its own messages unconditionally? Is the channel list explicit rather than "any channel I am in"? Does the run still stop at a pull request rather than merging? Does anything new reach for a credential that is not in 1Password? Merge only when all four are yes.

CHECKPOINTEither RESUME.md records a deferral with a trigger, or a merged pull request contains an explicit channel allowlist, an explicit app allowlist, an escalation rule, and a test proving the bot ignores its own posts in the allowlisted channel.

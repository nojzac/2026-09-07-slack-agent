SLACK AGENTS · CHAPTER "USING YOUR AGENT" · LESSON 18

What the "Using your agent" chapter is for

Ray's shortest video in the course — twenty-one seconds — and it is a hinge. Everything before it was about getting a bot to exist. Everything after it is about getting work out of it while you are not watching. Nothing is built in this lesson; what it asks for is a decision about how much of your work you are willing to stop triggering by hand.

Ray's video: 21 s This page: 10 min At the end: a decision about which of lessons 19–21 you actually want, and in what order

What you'll have at the end

Three ideas, in increasing order of what they ask you to give up. The third is the cheapest and is the one to do first.

  • A map of the chapter, so you can read lessons 19–21 knowing what each one is for.
  • A named list of what Joestar would have to give up for each idea — the guards, the credentials, the review gate.
  • An order, written down: which of the three to do, which to defer, and why.
  • No code. This lesson changes nothing in the repository, and neither do the next three unless you decide they should.

Who this is for, and what it assumes

You have a working bot: it answers in Slack, it can open a pull request, it has skills and a memory, and every run happens in a sandbox that is destroyed afterwards. In other words you finished the "0 to 1" chapter — lesson 17 was its last page. You have not yet had the bot do anything you did not personally ask for. That is the exact starting point this chapter assumes.

Why this lesson exists

Ray: "in the next few videos, we're gonna be going through a few ways in which we can use our Slack agents to take more work off our plate."

The gap this closes. A built agent and a useful agent are different things. The first is a technical achievement you can demo; the second changes what happens on a day when you do not open Slack. Ray spends twenty-one seconds on the transition because the transition is not technical — it is a decision about delegation that no amount of code will make for you.

PART 1

Understand what the chapter changes

Two things: what shifts when a course stops teaching you to build a tool and starts teaching you to hand work to it, and which specific parts of this bot the next three lessons want you to loosen.

From building the agent to operating itWhy the course splits here, and what the second half is actually teaching.

UNDERSTAND

Everything in lessons 04 to 17 answered a mechanical question: how does a Slack event reach a sandbox, how does a credential get there without landing in a file, how does the model learn a habit. Those questions have right answers, and you can tell when you got one wrong because something breaks.

The rest of the course has no such tell. "Should this bot investigate alerts on its own?" has no correct answer that testing will reveal. It has a your answer, which depends on what you own, what breaks, and how much you are willing to review.

You have finished training the new hire, and now you have to give them a job. The training had a syllabus. The job does not — it has a scope, a set of things they may decide alone, and a set of things they must bring to you. Getting that wrong is not a bug; it is a management mistake, and it fails quietly.
Ray: "in the next few videos, we're gonna be going through a few ways in which we can use our Slack agents to take more work off our plate and be more productive with them." That is the entire video. The chapter is three patterns, and he narrates each one from his own running business rather than building anything.

The two guards in this bot that the rest of the chapter argues withBoth are single lines in classifyEvent(), and both were put there deliberately.

UNDERSTAND

Joestar decides whether to answer an incoming Slack event in one pure function, classifyEvent() in joestar-agent/api/slack/events.js. Two of its decisions are exactly what lesson 19 asks you to reverse:

if (isFromBot(event, botUserId)) {
  return { action: 'skip', reason: 'posted by a bot — never answer ourselves' };
}
...
if (!event.thread_ts || event.thread_ts === event.ts) {
  return { action: 'skip', reason: 'channel chatter, not addressed to us' };
}
Alerts are bot messages, and alerts are channel chatter. A Sentry, Vercel or Trigger.dev notification trips both guards. An on-call agent is therefore not a feature you add beside the existing behaviour — it is a hole you cut in the two rules that currently stop this bot talking to itself in a loop. Lesson 19's page is mostly about how to cut that hole narrowly.

The first guard exists because the bot posts into channels it is listening to; without it, a reply is an event, which produces a reply. The second exists because the bot hears every message in every channel it is in — the message.channels subscription added in lesson 07 — and "hears everything" plus "answers everything" is a bot nobody can use.

The emergency procedure is already written. RESUME.md says that a second unprompted reply means turning off the message.channels subscription in Slack immediately, then fixing the guard. Anything in this chapter that makes the bot speak unprompted makes that procedure more likely to be needed, not less.
WHAT PART 1 SETTLEDThe chapter asks three questions, not three build tasks. Two of them cost this bot a loop guard or a scheduler; one costs a line of text.
PART 2

Decide

Three steps, all reading and writing prose. Nothing is committed to the agent's code in this lesson.

Read the chapter, then write down what you will actually doThe output is a paragraph in RESUME.md, not a pull request.

DO NOW

  1. Read all three pages before acting on any of them. Lesson 19, lesson 20, lesson 21. They compound: ownership delegation without an on-call trigger has nothing to react to, and both are much easier to tune once the bot is telling you what it lacks.

  2. Answer one question honestly: what currently pages you? If nothing in your world generates alerts on a regular basis, lesson 19 has nothing to react to and lesson 20 has no stream to own. Say so in writing rather than building the machinery and waiting for it to matter.

    Where this departs from Ray. Ray runs a business with Sentry, Trigger.dev and a lead pipeline all posting into Slack. His chapter assumes a steady flow of incoming trouble. If yours is a course project with no users, the honest version of lessons 19 and 20 is "understood, deferred", and that is a finished lesson, not a skipped one.

  3. Write the decision into RESUME.md — which of the three you are doing, in what order, and the condition that would change your mind. A deferral with a trigger ("when anything is live enough to page me") is a decision; a deferral without one is a thing you will re-litigate every time you open the folder.

CHECKPOINTRESUME.md contains a short paragraph naming which of lessons 19–21 you are doing and what would change that. No file in joestar-agent/ has changed.
